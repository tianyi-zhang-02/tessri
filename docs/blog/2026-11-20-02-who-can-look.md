# Who can look, held against who reviewed

The brief ends its first section with a sentence and up to three names: the people who can look at the change, the authors apart. For most of Tessri's life that list was ranked by each person's share of the changed files' history. It is a claim about people, so we tested it against people, on the merged pull requests of two public repositories. The list lost to a plain count, won on one repository and lost on the other against the busiest people, and found out that the strongest predictor of who reviews is something git cannot see. The list changed because of it, and this is what changed and why.

![On two repositories' pull requests, the share where at least one offered name actually reviewed: Tessri's list 0.47 and 0.59, a plain count of recent committers 0.46 and 0.61, the busiest committers 0.36 and 0.71, and the people who reviewed most before, whom git cannot see, 0.80 and 0.87.](figures/reviewers.svg)

## The claim

*Who can look* is a capacity question: not who should approve, but who is able to read this change with some knowledge of the code. Tessri answered it from the files' history. For each changed file, the people who held at least a tenth of its commits, weighted so that a commit a year old counts half, and who were still active; across the change, each person weighted by the lines of the files they held. Up to three names, the authors apart, with the sentence *Git cannot see who reviewed; these are the people who maintain the changed files.*

## The test

`tools/reviewer_match.py` has two halves. `collect` reads a repository's merged pull requests through the GitHub command line, newest first, keeping those whose merge commit the local checkout holds: the reviews, the reviewers GitHub lists as requested, and the git identities behind the commits. `score` is offline. For each pull request it moves a detached worktree to the merge commit and runs the triage of that change against its base, with the clock set to the merge time, so ownership and activity are what git knew when the change was merged and nothing from after it is read. The names triage offers are compared with the people who actually reviewed: a review in any state counts, the authors and bots do not.

Three other offers are scored the same way, so the list has something to lose to: the people who committed most to the changed files in the 365 days before the merge; the busiest committers of the whole repository in the 90 days before; and, from the review data git does not hold, the people who reviewed most often among the pull requests merged before this one. Logins are matched to git names through the commits' identities and the profiles' names, and an offered name that matches no login is dropped from the offer, which penalises the offers more than the baselines.

## The large repository

Three hundred merged pull requests of a large repository with a company's team behind it, 213 of them judged, read on 13 November 2026:

```text
Method                                            offered on  precision  recall  hit@1  any hit
triage: active holders of the changed files       195 of 213       0.17    0.33   0.18     0.41
committers to the changed files, last 365 days    199 of 213       0.17    0.37   0.27     0.45
busiest committers repo-wide, last 90 days        213 of 213       0.12    0.29   0.16     0.37
most frequent reviewers before this pull request  199 of 213       0.28    0.68   0.44     0.82
```

Three findings. Naming the people behind the files beats naming the busiest people: both file-level offers hit more often and more precisely than the repository's most active committers, so the capacity question was worth asking. The ownership list does not beat a plain count: the two agree on the first name about half the time and hit on different pull requests, and on the first name the count is better. And reviewing is a role: two people took 31% and 23% of all review slots, 56% of the judged pull requests were reviewed only by the three most frequent reviewers, and the list of who reviewed most before hit on 82% of the pull requests. The guide wrote down, at that point, that recency mattered more than share, and that this was a reason to change the ranking *once another repository said the same*.

## The small repository

The second repository was chosen to be different: small, old, merged with merge commits, maintained by a few core developers. Three hundred merged pull requests, 157 judged, read on 15 November 2026:

```text
Method                                            offered on  precision  recall  hit@1  any hit
triage: active holders of the changed files       147 of 157       0.30    0.46   0.30     0.58
committers to the changed files, last 365 days    148 of 157       0.27    0.52   0.33     0.61
busiest committers repo-wide, last 90 days        157 of 157       0.28    0.60   0.43     0.71
most frequent reviewers before this pull request  148 of 157       0.36    0.79   0.36     0.87
```

The plain count beats the share again, and the role replicates almost exactly: 30% and 24% of the slots to two people, 87% any hit for the past reviewers. One result reversed. Here four people write and review most of the code, so the busiest committers of the last 90 days are the reviewers whatever the files, and that offer won by a distance. Where a few people review everything, the right offer is those few; the page did not make it.

## What changed in the code

Two things, on 18 November. The list is now ordered by the number of commits each person made to the changed files in the last year, most first; the share of the files' history only orders the people who hold a file without a commit this year, and the sentence under the table says so. And when the repository's busiest committers of the last 90 days are not already on the list, the page names them, with their share of the window's commits, under the list.

Measured again as it ships, on both repositories, the list matches the plain count and keeps a little of the share's precision: on the large repository 0.17 precision, 0.39 recall, 0.47 any hit against 0.17, 0.39, 0.46 for the count; on the small one 0.28, 0.48, 0.59 against 0.27, 0.52, 0.61, and on the 148 pull requests where both offer, 0.29 precision against 0.27. The regulars line matches the busiest-committers baseline on both: 0.71 against 0.71 any hit on the small repository, 0.35 against 0.36 on the large.

## Two things that had to change to measure that

The first measurement of the regulars line scored 0.64 any hit on the small repository, not 0.71. The difference was merge commits: the page counted only commits that touch files, the baseline counted everything git lists, and in a merge-commit workflow the person who merges a pull request is very often the one who reviewed it. Merges count now, and the guide says why.

The first measurement on the large repository was worse in a more embarrassing way: the regulars' first name hit on 3% of the pull requests. The busiest committer there, by a margin, was a service account whose name ends in `-ci`. Tessri's rule for what counts as a bot caught `[bot]`, dependabot, renovate, github-actions and pre-commit-ci, and not that. A name or address ending in `-ci` or `-bot` is automation now, wherever Tessri reads history: such an account owns nothing and is offered to nobody. The measurement did what measurements are for; the fix reaches ownership, triage and the brief, not just the line it was found under.

## The role git cannot see

The strongest predictor on both repositories is the one Tessri cannot compute: the people who review most. Git's file history names who wrote the code, not who reads it, and on both repositories two people read about half of everything. That is why the sentence stays *git cannot see who reviewed*, why the brief offers rather than assigns, and why the past-reviewer baseline is printed beside the offers rather than quietly dropped. A host that knows its review history could do better than the page; the page says what it knows.

## A third repository, with nothing to judge

A TypeScript monorepo was the third run. Of its last three hundred merged pull requests, 112 changed no code file, and fifteen carried a review by someone other than the authors; the core team merges without recording reviews on GitHub. Fourteen judged pull requests are not a result, so the guide records the limit rather than a table. The backtest, which needs only history, ran on the same repository and held.

## What we would still like to know

Precision is the number to watch, since the share list won it and the reranked list gives up a little. Whether the reviewer was the right one is not in the data: a review by whoever was free counts as much as one by the person who knew the code. Identities are matched by name and address, and a contributor who commits under a name unlike their login drops out of the offers. The commands run on any repository with pull requests on GitHub; [the reviewer match](../guide/reviewer-match.html) has every number, including the first runs, and what the test does not measure.

```bash
python3 tools/reviewer_match.py collect owner/name path/to/checkout --out pulls.json
python3 tools/reviewer_match.py score pulls.json path/to/checkout
```
