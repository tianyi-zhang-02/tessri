# Who can look, held against who reviewed

The brief ends its first section with up to three names: the people who can look at the change, the authors apart. That is a claim about people, so it was tested against people, on the pull requests of two public repositories. The list changed because of what came back.

![On two repositories' pull requests, the share where at least one offered name actually reviewed: Tessri's list 0.47 and 0.59, a plain count of recent committers 0.46 and 0.61, the busiest committers 0.36 and 0.71, and the people who reviewed most before, whom git cannot see, 0.80 and 0.87.](figures/reviewers.svg)

## The test

`tools/reviewer_match.py` reads a repository's merged pull requests through the GitHub command line, then, offline, moves a worktree to each merge commit and runs the triage of that change against its base with the clock set to the merge time. The names triage offers are compared with the people who actually reviewed. Three other offers are scored the same way: the people who committed most to the changed files in the year before, the busiest committers of the whole repository in the 90 days before, and, from data git does not hold, the people who reviewed most often before that pull request.

## What came back, and what changed

The list used to be ranked by each person's share of the files' history, with a one-year half-life. On both repositories that list did not beat a plain count of who had committed to the same files in the last year; it hit on different pull requests, and on the first name it was worse. Recency matters more than share for this question. So the list is now ordered by commits to the changed files in the last year, most first, and the share only orders the people who hold a file without a commit this year. Measured again as it ships, it matches the plain count on both repositories and keeps a little of the share's precision.

The small repository added a second lesson. Four people write and review most of its code, so the busiest committers of the last 90 days were the best offer there by a distance, where on the large repository they were the worst. The page now names the repository's regulars under the list when they are not already on it, with their share of the window's commits, merges included: in a merge-commit workflow the person who merged is often the one who reviewed.

## What git cannot see

On both repositories two people took about half of all review slots, and the list of who reviewed most before hit on 80% and 87% of the pull requests. Reviewing is a role, and git's file history cannot name the people who hold it. That is why the sentence on the page reads *Git cannot see who reviewed; these are the people who committed to the changed files in the last year*, and why the brief offers rather than assigns.

A third repository, a TypeScript monorepo, had nothing to judge: fifteen of its last three hundred merged pull requests carried a review by someone other than the authors. The test needs a repository that reviews on GitHub; it says so instead of printing a table on fourteen rows.
